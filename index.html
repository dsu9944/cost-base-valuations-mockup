Why it matters

The ATO formula assumes a straight line. Markets don’t.

When part of your gain is taxed as if the property grew smoothly to a split date — especially the 30 June 2027 spike — a contemporaneous signed market value can differ from waiting until 2035 and reconstructing with a formula.

Formula vs signed market value

The default path leans on a straight-line assumption between known points. Real neighbourhoods move in fits: booms, flat years, local shocks. Locking an AVI-signed market value as at the tax date brands the date that splits your gain — while evidence is still current.

Contemporaneous 2027 > reconstruct in 2035

Ordering closer to the date means comps, condition, and context are still available. Waiting a decade invites thinner evidence and more argument about what the market “must have been.”

Example

Simple numbers (illustrative)

Suppose a home was worth $600k at purchase and $1.2m at a later sale. A straight-line formula to a mid-period tax date might imply ~$900k at that date. If the local market had already jumped earlier, a signed market value as at the tax date might sit closer to $1.05m — changing how much of the gain sits on each side of the split.

Not tax advice. Your accountant applies any signed valuation within your own facts, elections, and ATO-process work. Figures here are made-up to show the idea.
