Draft formula vs market valuation

Unless you choose the Government’s draft apportioning formula, the law uses your property’s market value at the tax date (the point just before the new CGT rules start).

Instead of market value, the law lets you choose a Government apportioning method when you sell. It’s still a Treasury draft and may change. As drafted, it applies to real property, works backwards from your sale price, and assumes the property grew at the same compounding rate every day you owned it. It isn’t straight-line.

I’m not selling. Why would I get one?

You don’t have to. A market valuation at the tax date records the value while sales evidence is current. You can instead choose the Government’s draft apportioning formula when you sell. Your accountant decides how any valuation is used. The ATO may disagree with any valuation. Not tax advice.

Isn’t there a formula instead of a valuation?

The law lets you choose a Government apportioning method instead of market value when you sell. That method is still a draft. It works backwards from your sale price, assuming the same compounding growth every day you owned the property, so it can’t be worked out until you sell. It may put more or less of your gain after the tax date than a valuation would. Your accountant can compare both. Not tax advice.

How is this different from Domain or REA?

They usually estimate “today.” We frame value as at a CGT date for NSW & VIC residential. Free = indicative. Paid = signed by a qualified valuer your accountant can consider for tax records. The ATO (or others) may not accept or may disagree with a valuation. Not tax advice.

What if I moved out and rented?

If it was your main residence and you first rented it out after 20 August 1996, the tax law can treat you as buying it at its market value on the day it was first rented. That doesn’t apply in every case — your accountant will confirm. We can value as at a past date (a retrospective valuation). Not tax advice.
